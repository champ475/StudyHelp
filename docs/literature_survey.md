# Literature Survey — Real-Time, Point-of-Error Tutoring for Primary-School Math
### Prepared for: StudyHelp (Class 5 CBSE/NCERT math, step-by-step Socratic tutor)

## Scope and method

This survey covers five overlapping research threads that together map the space around the StudyHelp idea: (A) classic and modern Intelligent Tutoring System (ITS) architectures and student knowledge modeling, (B) automated diagnosis of math misconceptions ("buggy rules" through to LLM-based classification), (C) LLM-based and Socratic dialogue tutoring systems, (D) educational-psychology research on feedback timing (immediate vs. delayed), and (E) LLM reliability/hallucination/grounding and child-safety research relevant to using LLMs with young learners. Every paper below was located and verified through live web search against arXiv, ACL Anthology, IEEE/ACM/Springer/Nature publisher pages, OpenReview, or ERIC — nothing here is fabricated; where a lead could not be fully verified it's flagged rather than written up as a confirmed source. Given how fast this specific area (LLM tutoring) is moving, recency is weighted heavily for threads C and E (mostly 2023–2026), while threads A and B necessarily include foundational work from the 1970s–90s that the modern papers still build on and cite.

A note on "impact factor": the highest-impact venues in this specific field are not classic IF-ranked journals so much as the field's own top venues — *International Journal of Artificial Intelligence in Education* (IJAIED), *IEEE Transactions on Learning Technologies*, *Computers & Education*, *Review of Educational Research* (very high IF, ~8–10, in education broadly), and top NLP/ML venues (NeurIPS, ACL, EMNLP, NAACL, ICLR) for the LLM-tutoring work, since most of the cutting-edge material in 2024–2026 is arXiv/conference-published rather than traditional journal-published. I've prioritized these plus a 2025 *npj Science of Learning* (Nature portfolio) systematic review, which is one of the strongest single citations in the whole survey.

## A. Intelligent Tutoring Systems (ITS) and Knowledge Tracing

The throughline in this section: every generation of student-modeling technique gets better at predicting *whether* a student will answer correctly, but essentially none of them — classic or modern — natively diagnose *which specific misconception* caused a wrong step, or intervene *while* the student is still mid-step. That gap is the opening for StudyHelp.

**A1. Corbett & Anderson (1994).** "Knowledge Tracing: Modeling the Acquisition of Procedural Knowledge." *User Modeling and User-Adapted Interaction*, 4(4), 253–278. [link](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF01099821)
Introduced Bayesian Knowledge Tracing (BKT) — a per-skill Hidden Markov Model (prior/learn/guess/slip parameters) updated after each attempt, originally built for the ACT Programming Tutor. Became the basis of mastery-based practice sequencing in Cognitive Tutors. *Gap:* binary skill states only, no misconception-specific diagnosis, updates only after a scored attempt rather than mid-step.

**A2. Koedinger & Corbett (2006).** "Cognitive Tutors: Technology Bringing Learning Sciences to the Classroom." In *The Cambridge Handbook of the Learning Sciences*. [link](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~aleven/Papers/2006/KoedingerCorbett2006.pdf)
Describes "model tracing" — an ACT-R production-rule system encoding both correct strategies and known buggy rules, matching every student action in real time against the model and generating per-step feedback. Deployed to ~500,000 students by 2004–05, with reported 50–100% problem-solving score gains. *This is the closest classic architectural precedent to StudyHelp's core mechanic* — real-time, step-level matching against a misconception library — but its feedback was templated hints, not open Socratic dialogue, and authoring buggy-rule libraries by hand was labor-intensive and domain-specific.

**A3. Piech et al. (2015).** "Deep Knowledge Tracing." *NeurIPS 2015* / [arXiv:1506.05908](https://arxiv.org/abs/1506.05908)
Replaced BKT's per-skill HMM with a single LSTM ingesting the full (skill, correctness) sequence. Large AUC gains over BKT but a black box — no interpretability, no misconception typing, no real-time-interruption mechanism. Spawned a large family of successors:

- **DKVMN** (Zhang et al., WWW 2017, [link](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3038912.3052580)) — memory-augmented network with key/value memory for latent concepts, somewhat more interpretable than DKT's hidden state, but latent concepts aren't guaranteed to align with real misconceptions.
- **SAKT** (Pandey & Karypis, EDM 2019 / [arXiv:1907.06837](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.06837)) — self-attention instead of recurrence, notably better on *sparse* interaction histories, which matters since a single Class-5 tutoring session is much shorter than typical KT benchmarks.
- **SAINT+** (Shin et al., LAK 2021, [link](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3448139.3448188)) — Transformer encoder-decoder adding response-time features; needs large-scale training data (hundreds of millions of interactions), likely impractical to train from scratch for a narrow Class-5 domain.
- **AKT** (Ghosh, Heffernan & Lan, KDD 2020 / [arXiv:2007.12324](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12324)) — attention with forgetting-curve decay and Rasch-model regularization for more interpretable embeddings; not evaluated on K-12 arithmetic specifically.

**A4. Shen et al. (2024).** "A Survey of Knowledge Tracing: Models, Variants, and Applications." *IEEE Transactions on Learning Technologies*, 17, 1898–1919 / [arXiv:2105.15106](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.15106)
The field's umbrella survey and taxonomy (probabilistic / logistic-factor / deep-learning families), with reusable open-source implementations (EduKTM). Useful as the "state of the field" reference to benchmark any new component against, but its datasets skew MOOC/college-prep, not elementary.

**A5. Bai et al. (2024).** "A Survey of Explainable Knowledge Tracing." *Applied Intelligence* (Springer) / [arXiv:2403.07279](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.07279)
Reviews interpretability across transparent (BKT/IRT) vs. black-box (deep) KT models. Finds no standardized evaluation for explainable KT and notes technical interpretability methods remain inaccessible to non-expert stakeholders like classroom teachers — directly relevant to StudyHelp needing to be explainable both to the child (the Socratic dialogue *is* an explanation) and to parents/teachers monitoring progress.

**A6. Mao, Lin & Chi (2018).** "Deep Learning vs. Bayesian Knowledge Tracing: Student Models for Interventions." *Journal of Educational Data Mining*, 10(2), 28–54. [link](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/view/318)
Head-to-head BKT vs. LSTM comparison on two real ITS deployments; found that actionable, reasonably accurate predictions are possible from *partial* interaction sequences (as little as 50–70% of a session) — useful evidence that a system can plausibly decide "an error is happening" without needing to observe a full completed session first.

**A7. Létourneau et al. (2025).** "A Systematic Review of AI-Driven Intelligent Tutoring Systems (ITS) in K-12 Education." *npj Science of Learning*, 10(1), 29. [link](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41539-025-00320-7)
**One of the two or three most load-bearing citations in this entire survey.** Systematic review of 28 quasi-experimental K-12 ITS studies (n=4,597). ITS shows generally positive but modest effects vs. traditional instruction, shrinking further against non-intelligent digital alternatives. Critically: only 14% of studies involved elementary-age students and *none* involved preschool; **no reviewed study addressed real-time error detection or misconception diagnosis capability**; no article discussed AI ethics. This is a recent, high-quality, citable confirmation that both "elementary-age learners" and "real-time point-of-error diagnosis" are genuinely underexplored in the existing ITS literature — precisely the two axes StudyHelp combines.

**A8. Shih, Chang, Kuo & Huang (2023).** "Mathematics Intelligent Tutoring System for Learning Multiplication and Division of Fractions Based on Diagnostic Teaching." *Education and Information Technologies*, 28(7), 9189–9210. [link](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-022-11553-z)
**The closest real precedent by age and domain.** A dialogue-based ITS for 6th graders (~11–12, adjacent to Class 5) using "diagnostic teaching" pedagogy — provoking cognitive conflict, simplification, representational teaching — against a misconception library built from 290 real students' errors. Quasi-experimental study (n=66) found significant gains (F=5.52, p<.05), concentrated among lower-performing students, with 91.4% enjoyment and 82.9% of students feeling it resembled human teaching. Its own stated limitations are directly useful for StudyHelp's design: keyboard/mouse input was "time-consuming and inconvenient" for young learners, and rigid block/pattern matching couldn't capture all valid calculation orders for word problems — both point toward StudyHelp's choice of structured-but-flexible step input and a step-graph (not linear list) representation.

**A9. Mitton et al. (2026).** "Misconception Diagnosis From Student-Tutor Dialogue: Generate, Retrieve, Rerank." [arXiv:2602.02414](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02414)
A three-stage pipeline (LLM generates candidate misconceptions → embedding retrieval narrows candidates → LLM reranks), built on the Eedi misconception taxonomy. A fine-tuned 7B open model beat a much larger closed model on misconception-ranking accuracy. *Very recent preprint (2026), not yet peer-reviewed* — flag accordingly. Diagnoses from *completed* dialogue turns, not live mid-solution. This is one of the closest existing technical blueprints for StudyHelp's "retrieve the matching misconception entry" component (see also section B12 and C15 below — this paper is directly relevant to three of the five threads).

## B. Automated Diagnosis of Math Misconceptions: From "Buggy Rules" to LLMs

**B1. Brown & Burton (1978).** "Diagnostic Models for Procedural Bugs in Basic Mathematical Skills." *Cognitive Science*, 2(2), 155–192. [link](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdf/10.1207/s15516709cog0202_4)
The **DEBUGGY** system — the foundational demonstration that children's subtraction errors are often systematic, reproducible "bugs" (e.g. "smaller-from-larger" regardless of position), not random slips, diagnosable from a hand-coded catalogue of ~100 bugs. Offline/batch only, needs many problems from one student, doesn't generalize past subtraction. This is the intellectual ancestor of the "buggy rule library" idea recommended in StudyHelp's architecture doc.

**B2. Brown & VanLehn (1980).** "Repair Theory: A Generative Theory of Bugs in Procedural Skills." *Cognitive Science*, 4, 379–426. [link](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1207/s15516709cog0404_3)
Rather than hand-enumerating bugs, *generates* them: a correct procedure with a step deleted reaches an "impasse," and generic repair heuristics route around it, producing predictable bugs. Gives a mechanistic account of *why* a student produces a specific wrong step at the moment of impasse — directly useful for framing what "the moment of the mistake" actually is, cognitively, in StudyHelp's real-time trigger.

**B3. VanLehn (1990).** *Mind Bugs: The Origins of Procedural Misconceptions.* MIT Press. [publisher link](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262512909/mind-bugs/)
Book-length extension of repair theory into the Sierra system, modeling how children *acquire* (not just exhibit) buggy procedures from instruction. Establishes that misconceptions are structured and predictable — the theoretical grounding for building StudyHelp's taxonomy around anticipated misconceptions rather than generic error flags.

**B4. Feldman, Cho, Ong, Gulwani, Popović & Andersen (2018).** "Automatic Diagnosis of Students' Misconceptions in K-8 Mathematics." *CHI 2018*. [link](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/~molly/chi2018.pdf)
A modern, K-8-explicit successor to Brown & Burton using **program synthesis** — reconstructs a student's likely buggy "thought process" as executable pseudocode from multiple solved problems. Replicated 70% of Ashlock's expert-curated subtraction misconceptions, generated valid buggy programs for 86% of real student problem sets. *Not real-time* (1–130 seconds, needs several problems, not a single in-progress step) and assumes consistent bug application across problems. Directly relevant as a technical approach to automatic buggy-rule inference, but the point-of-error-in-a-single-step gap is exactly what it doesn't close.

**B5. Gorgun & Botelho (2023).** "Enhancing the Automatic Identification of Common Math Misconceptions Using Natural Language Processing." *AIED 2023, Posters/LBR*. [link](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-36336-8_47)
Sentence-BERT embeddings + clustering to distinguish systematic "misconceptions" from random wrong answers via commonality of incorrect responses. Promising but interpretability of the *specific* misconception is still weak, and it's not shown as real-time.

**B6. Wang et al. (2020) / the Eedi dataset.** "Instructions and Guide for Diagnostic Questions: The NeurIPS 2020 Education Challenge." [arXiv:2007.12061](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12061); later reused as the 2024 Kaggle "Eedi – Mining Misconceptions in Mathematics" challenge.
A large real-world dataset of UK-classroom multiple-choice diagnostic questions where every wrong distractor maps to a named misconception — hundreds of expert-labeled misconception classes, expert-grounded in real primary/secondary classroom data. Probably **the most important existing dataset resource** for grounding a primary-math misconception taxonomy, though it's pre-authored multiple-choice, not free step-by-step solving.

**B7. Otero, Druga & Lan (2025).** "A Benchmark for Math Misconceptions: Bridging Gaps in Middle School Algebra with AI-Supported Instruction." *Discover Education*, 4, 277. [link](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44217-025-00742-w)
A 55-misconception, 220-example benchmark from 145 peer-reviewed sources, teacher-validated (5 middle-school teachers). GPT-4-turbo scored only 52.96% unconstrained accuracy at identifying the correct misconception, jumping to 73.82–83.91% when the topic was constrained. **A concrete, quantified caution**: off-the-shelf LLM misconception diagnosis is roughly coin-flip-to-two-thirds accurate unless scoped to a known taxonomy — direct support for StudyHelp's decision to retrieve from a curated, topic-keyed misconception bank rather than let the LLM freely diagnose.

**B8. Jin et al. (2024).** "Investigating Large Language Models in Diagnosing Students' Cognitive Skills in Math Problem-Solving." *COLM 2024*. [arXiv:2504.00843](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.00843v1)
639 real student responses, TIMSS-style cognitive-skill annotations, 16 LLMs tested (GPT/Claude/Gemini/Llama/DeepSeek families). **All 16 models scored F1 < 0.5** on diagnosing *why* a student went wrong, with strong overconfidence specifically on incorrect diagnoses. Grades 7–9, not primary, but an important negative result: general-purpose LLMs are currently unreliable diagnosticians of the cognitive cause of a math error — reinforcing the case for specialized taxonomies/retrieval over naive prompting.

**B9. Ross & Andreas (2025).** "Learning to Make MISTAKEs: Modeling Incorrect Student Thinking and Key Errors." [arXiv:2510.11502](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.11502)
Trains models to simulate misconception-consistent wrong answers, classify misconceptions from wrong answers, and generate plausible distractors, using cycle-consistency between answers and latent misconceptions. Relevant as a way to generate synthetic training data for rarer Class-5 misconceptions where real labeled examples are scarce.

**B10. Song et al. (2026).** "Can MLLMs Read Students' Minds? Unpacking Multimodal Error Analysis in Handwritten Math." [arXiv:2603.24961](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24961)
Introduces "ScratchMath," 1,720 authentic handwritten primary/middle-school samples (Chinese curriculum), testing 16 multimodal LLMs on classifying/explaining errors from images. Large gap vs. human experts; models default to an "examinee" mindset (trying to re-solve correctly) rather than a "diagnostician" mindset (explaining why the *student's* answer is wrong) — a named failure mode any system using an LLM for diagnosis needs to explicitly counteract via prompting/role-framing. **The most grade-appropriate paper found in this thread** (primary/middle-school, explicit error taxonomy) even though it's post-hoc (completed handwritten work) rather than live.

**B11. Sun, Yin, Huang, Qiu & Zhao (2025).** "Error Classification of Large Language Models on Math Word Problems: A Dynamically Adaptive Framework." *EMNLP 2025 Findings* / [arXiv:2501.15581](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.15581)
An automated, emergent (not fixed) error taxonomy built by clustering GPT-4o's own explanations of incorrect solutions. Methodologically interesting (the "cluster LLM-explained errors" technique could be adapted from diagnosing model errors to diagnosing child errors), but this paper studies *LLM-generated* errors, not human/child errors — relevance is indirect.

**B12. Mitton et al. (2026)** — see A9 above; also directly relevant here as the clearest modern "generate → retrieve → rerank" misconception-diagnosis pipeline.

## C. LLM-Based and Socratic Dialogue Tutoring Systems

This is the most active and directly relevant thread, and the richest source of both close precedents and explicit gaps.

**C1. Macina et al. (2023).** "MathDial: A Dialogue Tutoring Dataset with Rich Pedagogical Properties Grounded in Math Reasoning Problems." *EMNLP 2023 Findings*. [arXiv:2305.14536](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14536)
**The field's standard benchmark/dataset** — 3,000 human-teacher + LLM-simulated-student tutoring dialogues on GSM8K-style word problems, annotated with a "teacher moves" taxonomy. Showed off-the-shelf GPT-3 fails as a tutor (factually wrong feedback, premature answer reveal); fine-tuning on MathDial improves the success/leakage trade-off. Nearly every other paper in this thread trains or evaluates on it. Dialogues are simulated, not real classroom deployment, and skew middle/upper-elementary word problems rather than confirmed age-10 content.

**C2. Ding et al. (2024).** "Boosting Large Language Models with Socratic Method for Conversational Mathematics Teaching" (SocraticMath). *CIKM 2024*. [arXiv:2407.17349](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.17349)
A staged Socratic dialogue manager (review → guidance/heuristic → rectification → summarization) with a new SocraticMATH dataset. Shifts model behavior toward guided discovery. No explicit point-of-mistake interruption mechanism, not evaluated on young children.

**C3. Liu et al. (2024).** "SocraticLM: Exploring Socratic Personalized Teaching with Large Language Models." *NeurIPS 2024 (Spotlight)*. [OpenReview](https://openreview.net/forum?id=qkoZgJhxsA)
(Note: a second, unrelated "SocraticLM" system — disambiguate carefully when citing.) A "Dean–Teacher–Student" multi-agent pipeline synthesizing a 35K-dialogue dataset (SocraTeach) and fine-tuning models to ask guiding questions across six simulated student personas; outperforms GPT-4 by >12% on teaching-performance metrics. **Notably, the paper describes no explicit verification/grounding mechanism** to guarantee mathematical correctness before Socratic questioning begins — the closest architectural comparison to StudyHelp, and useful precisely because it exposes the gap StudyHelp's symbolic-verification layer is designed to close.

**C4. Macina et al. (2025).** "MathTutorBench: A Benchmark for Measuring Open-Ended Pedagogical Capabilities of LLM Tutors." *EMNLP 2025 (Oral)*. [arXiv:2502.18940](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.18940)
A benchmark suite (math expertise, student-understanding — including *mistake location* — and pedagogy) plus a learned reward model. Key finding: **math-solving ability does not transfer to teaching ability**, and pedagogical quality degrades over longer dialogues. Its "mistake location" task relies on one annotated error point per problem, missing cascading errors — an explicit caveat relevant to how StudyHelp should handle compound mistakes.

**C5. Wang, Zhang, Robinson, Loeb & Demszky (2024).** "Bridging the Novice-Expert Gap via Models of Decision-Making: A Case Study on Remediating Math Mistakes." *NAACL 2024*. [arXiv:2310.10648](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.10648)
Encodes expert-tutor cognition as a 3-step decision framework (identify error → select remediation strategy → form instructional intent) before generating a response. GPT-4 conditioned on expert decisions was preferred 76% more than unconditioned; random decisions cut quality by 97%. **This is essentially the exact pipeline StudyHelp proposes** (diagnose → select strategy → generate Socratic response), with strong empirical evidence that the diagnose-then-strategize step is what makes the difference, not just prompting an LLM to "be Socratic."

**C6. Wang, Ribeiro, Robinson, Loeb & Demszky (2024).** "Tutor CoPilot: A Human-AI Approach for Scaling Real-Time Expertise." Stanford/EdWorkingPaper preprint. [link](https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai24_1054_v2.pdf)
**The strongest empirical evidence in this entire survey.** A preregistered RCT: 900 tutors, 1,800 K-12 students (grades 3–8, largely Title I/economically disadvantaged), real-time AI suggestions (Bridge-style) surfaced to *human* tutors mid-session. Treatment students were 4pp more likely to pass exit tickets (p<.01), with the effect concentrated among novice tutors (+9pp); treatment tutors asked more guiding questions and gave away answers less often. No significant effect on end-of-year standardized tests, though. This is the only paper in the whole survey with a real RCT and real learning-outcome data from students in the target age range, even though it's human-tutor-augmenting rather than direct student-facing AI.

**C7. Scarlatos, Baraniuk, Liu, Lee & Lan (2025).** "Training LLM-Based Tutors to Improve Student Learning Outcomes in Dialogues." *AIED 2025*. [arXiv:2503.06424](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.06424v2)
Two-stage fine-tuning (distill GPT-4o behavior, then DPO using preference pairs scored by a knowledge-tracing model predicting next-turn correctness + a pedagogical rubric). 33% improvement in predicted student correctness over GPT-4o at matched pedagogical quality. Authors explicitly caution the evaluation uses a *simulated* student-response predictor, not real students.

**C8. Petukhova, Nguyen & Kochmar (2026).** "Towards Pedagogically Aligned LLM Tutors for Math Mistake Remediation." *BEA Workshop 2026*. [arXiv:2606.21502](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.21502)
SFT+DPO alignment across five pedagogical dimensions (factuality, mistake identification, targetedness, answer-revealing, clarity). **The single most direct empirical validation of StudyHelp's core architectural bet**: models given an *externally supplied correctness flag and gold solution* generate substantially better remediation dialogue than models left to determine correctness themselves. Explicitly operates in reactive mode (after the full solution is submitted) rather than mid-step — a direct contrast case worth citing as "existing systems evaluate the final answer; ours intervenes mid-step."

**C9. Dinucu-Jianu et al. (2025).** "From Problem-Solving to Teaching Problem-Solving: Aligning LLMs with Pedagogy Using Reinforcement Learning." [arXiv:2505.15607](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.15607)
Online RL (not offline DPO) with a tunable "pedagogical-accuracy trade-off" penalty; a 7B model matches larger proprietary tutors while better preserving general reasoning ability than SFT alone. Useful as an alternative fine-tuning approach if StudyHelp eventually trains its own dialogue model rather than prompting a frontier model.

**C10. Lee, Shin, Jeong et al. (2026).** "LLMs Are Already Good Tutors: Training-Free Prompt Optimization for Pedagogical Math Tutoring." [arXiv:2605.27088](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.27088)
Argues fine-tuning is unnecessary — evolves system prompts only, including a "LeakShield" technique specifically targeting answer-leakage prevention. Matches or beats RL-trained baselines with ~100x less data and single-GPU inference. **Directly actionable for StudyHelp's POC stage**: a low-cost, prompt-engineering-only path to reducing answer-leakage without needing custom fine-tuning infrastructure — highly relevant given the constrained-input, structured-step design already planned.

**C11. Gupta, Reddig, Calò, Weitekamp & MacLellan (2025).** "Beyond Final Answers: Evaluating Large Language Models for Math Tutoring." *AIED 2025*. [link](https://tail.cc.gatech.edu/publications/gupta-aied-2025)
LLMs solved 85.5% of college-algebra problems correctly, but only 56.6% of tutoring dialogues were entirely error-free (even though 90% were rated "high quality" overall). Authors conclude LLMs are "not yet suitable as intelligent tutors for math without human oversight or additional mechanisms to ensure correctness" — a striking solve-vs-teach gap and a direct citation supporting why StudyHelp shouldn't let the LLM freely tutor without the symbolic verification gate.

**C12. Srivatsa, Maurya & Kochmar (2025).** "LLMs Cannot Spot Math Errors, Even When Allowed to Peek Into the Solution." *EMNLP 2025 (Main)*. [link](https://aclanthology.org/2025.emnlp-main.553.pdf)
**Arguably the single most load-bearing citation for StudyHelp's core premise.** Tests whether LLMs can identify the *first* erroneous step in a stepwise student solution across 1,002 + 80,000 incorrect stepwise solutions (VtG, PRM800K). SOTA LLMs (Llama-3.1-405B, GPT-4o) perform poorly at first-error localization *even with the correct answer provided*; problem-solving ability does not predict error-detection ability; matching the student's own approach matters more than having the right answer. This is strong, recent, top-venue evidence that "detecting exactly where the mistake happened" is a distinct and currently unsolved LLM capability — the exact justification for using a deterministic verifier rather than an LLM to find the error.

**C13. Parra, Corica & Godoy (2026).** "Insights on the Pedagogical Abilities of AI-Powered Tutors in Math Dialogues." *Information* (MDPI), 17(1), 51. [link](https://www.mdpi.com/2078-2489/17/1/51)
NLP-feature analysis (readability, sentiment, uptake) comparing LLM vs. human tutor responses on the BEA 2025 shared-task dataset (2,480+ responses, 7 LLMs). **LLM responses require a higher reading level than human tutor responses** — a quantified warning that off-the-shelf LLM tutoring language may be too advanced for a 10-year-old reader, and that "uptake" (responding to what the student actually said) is the strongest differentiator of good guidance — directly informs prompt design for age-appropriate dialogue.

**C14. Peng, Yuan et al. (2025).** "KELE: A Multi-Agent Framework for Structured Socratic Teaching with Large Language Models." *EMNLP 2025 Findings*. [link](https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-emnlp.888.pdf)
A two-agent architecture (a "consultant" diagnosing cognitive state + selecting a strategy, and a "teacher" generating the dialogue turn), constrained by a rule system with 5 stages / 34 teaching strategies. A fine-tuned 9B model outperformed GPT-4o on teaching-specific metrics — evidence that a targeted diagnose-then-teach split beats raw model scale. Domain is elementary *science*, not math, but the two-agent architectural template is directly transferable.

**C15. Mitton et al. (2026)** — see A9/B12. Cross-relevant here specifically for the "generate candidate misconceptions → retrieve → rerank" pipeline as a diagnosis component that a Socratic dialogue generator could sit on top of.

## D. Educational Psychology: Feedback Timing, Scaffolding, and Productive Struggle

**D1. Kulik & Kulik (1988).** "Timing of Feedback and Verbal Learning." *Review of Educational Research*, 58(1), 79–97. [link](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.3102/00346543058001079)
Classic meta-analysis (~53 studies). Laboratory verbal-learning studies favored *delayed* feedback; applied classroom studies favored *immediate or equal* results. Established the field's central "it depends on setting" finding — instructional/applied contexts (like a math tutor) lean toward immediacy being fine or better, contrary to naive lab-based intuition.

**D2. Hattie & Timperley (2007).** "The Power of Feedback." *Review of Educational Research*, 77(1), 81–112. [link](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.3102/003465430298487)
Highly cited theoretical model: feedback works by answering "Where am I going / How am I going / Where to next" across task/process/self-regulation/self levels, with task- and process-level feedback outperforming praise or self-level feedback. Provides the theoretical justification for StudyHelp targeting the *specific misconception* (process level) rather than a bare correctness flag.

**D3. Shute (2008).** "Focus on Formative Feedback." *Review of Educational Research*, 78(1), 153–189. [link](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/0034654307313795)
Reviews 100+ studies; finds no consistent main effect of timing — it interacts with learner ability and task type. Immediate feedback tends to help low-achieving students and difficult/procedural tasks; delayed feedback tends to help high achievers and concept-formation/transfer tasks. About a third of feedback interventions in the reviewed literature showed *negative* effects. Directly supports designing StudyHelp's feedback to be immediate but carefully scaffolded and specific (appropriate for its target learners on a difficult task) — while explicitly warning that immediacy alone isn't automatically better.

**D4. Kandemir, Esposito, Gurgand & Ramus (2026).** "A Meta-Analysis of the Impact of Feedback Timing on Learning Outcomes in Computer-Assisted Learning." *Educational Psychology Review*, 38, Article 13. [link](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-026-10117-8)
**The first direct update to Kulik & Kulik since 1988**, specifically for computer-assisted learning — 51 studies, 160 effect sizes, meta-regression (1988–2024). Overall, timing shows **no significant average effect** (g=0.03), but with significant heterogeneity by educational level, domain, and response-time constraints. A genuinely important, very recent, high-quality caution: "immediate is simply better" is not an empirically settled fact even in digital learning specifically — which is exactly why studying this question for Class-5 math specifically (rather than assuming the answer) is a real contribution, not a foregone conclusion.

**D5. Brummer, de Boer, Mouw & Strijbos (2024).** "A Meta-Analysis of the Effects of Context, Content, and Task Factors of Digitally Delivered Instructional Feedback on Learning Performance." *Learning Environments Research*, 27(3), 453–476. [link](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10984-024-09501-4)
116 interventions, 46 studies. Overall moderate feedback effect (g=0.41); delayed *slightly* outperformed immediate on average, but *inconsistent/mixed* timing was worst of all — consistency of timing policy may matter more than the specific immediate-vs-delayed choice. Process-focused (g=0.38) and especially metacognition-targeted feedback (g=0.88) were the strongest content factors, and calculation-type tasks showed strong effects (g=0.76) — directly supports StudyHelp's process/metacognitive Socratic framing over a bare right/wrong flag.

**D6. Kehrer, Kelly & Heffernan (2013).** "Does Immediate Feedback While Doing Homework Improve Learning?" *FLAIRS 2013*. [link](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED615325.pdf)
A real RCT (n=61, 7th grade, ASSISTments platform) — immediate feedback produced significantly higher learning gains than delayed (next-day) feedback on real math homework (d=0.37, p=.015). One of the closest RCTs in spirit to StudyHelp (real classroom math, digital platform, age adjacent), though it only tests bare correctness feedback, not diagnostic Socratic dialogue.

**D7. Metcalfe, Kornell & Finn (2009).** "Delayed Versus Immediate Feedback in Children's and Adults' Vocabulary Learning." *Memory & Cognition*, 37(8), 1077–1087. [link](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/MC.37.8.1077)
Multi-session study, Grade 6 children (~11–12) and college students, vocabulary learning. **For children, delayed feedback produced better final learning than immediate**, even controlling for lag-to-test. **An important counter-finding worth engaging with directly** — the authors themselves note this is a declarative/verbal recall task, not procedural problem-solving, which is a real boundary condition to discuss: StudyHelp is correcting an active problem-solving *process* while a mistake is being made, not testing delayed recall of a fact, which plausibly changes which timing effect dominates — but this should be argued explicitly in the paper, not assumed away.

**D8. VanLehn (2011).** "The Relative Effectiveness of Human Tutoring, Intelligent Tutoring Systems, and Other Tutoring Systems." *Educational Psychologist*, 46(4), 197–221. [link](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00461520.2011.611369)
**Perhaps the single most directly relevant classic paper in the whole survey.** A meta-review categorizing tutoring systems by feedback granularity — answer-based (feedback only at the final answer), step-based, and substep-based (fine-grained, within-step). Finds human tutoring's effect size was lower than long assumed (d≈0.79, not 2.0), and that step-/substep-based systems approach human-tutor effectiveness, clearly outperforming answer-based systems. This is essentially the pre-LLM evidence base for "interrupt at the point of error/step rather than at the end" — functionally the mechanism StudyHelp implements.

**D9. Shih et al. (2023)** — see A8. Relevant here too as a real quasi-experimental study of near-Class-5-age students receiving real-time diagnostic feedback.

**D10. Young, Bevan & Sanders (2024).** "How Productive Is the Productive Struggle? Lessons Learned from a Scoping Review." *International Journal of Education in Mathematics, Science, and Technology*, 12(2), 470–495. [link](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1413403.pdf)
Scoping review (45 studies, 2011–2020) of K-12 "productive struggle." Frames the exact tension StudyHelp must navigate: struggle with a challenging task, properly supported, can build persistence and satisfaction, but the review explicitly finds the literature does **not** yet clarify feedback timing or exactly when an outside agent should intervene during struggle vs. let it continue. This is a genuine, explicitly-acknowledged open gap in the field — good to cite as motivation, and a caution that StudyHelp's "always interrupt immediately" default is a real design hypothesis to test (matching the three-condition study design already planned), not an established best practice.

*(A dedicated search for HCI literature on "interruption cost/benefit in digital learning tools" specifically — as distinct from workplace-interruption research — did not turn up a strong, directly on-topic match; this itself is worth noting in the paper as a further gap rather than papering over with a loosely-related workplace-interruption citation.)*

## E. LLM Reliability, Grounding, and Child-Specific Safety

This thread directly supports StudyHelp's central architectural bet: never let the LLM alone be the arbiter of mathematical correctness.

**E1. Zhou, Staats, Li, Szegedy, Weinberger & Wu (2024).** "Don't Trust: Verify — Grounding LLM Quantitative Reasoning with Autoformalization." *ICLR 2024*. [link](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/file/0a79ecda13603817de4cdfc68b417e89-Paper-Conference.pdf)
**The closest existing analog to StudyHelp's exact verification philosophy**, applied to formal theorem-proving. Auto-translates informal LLM solutions into Isabelle theorem-prover code, mechanically checks logical consistency, and votes only over verified solutions. +12% over plain majority voting on quantitative reasoning benchmarks. Directly citable prior art for "don't trust the LLM's self-assessment of correctness — verify externally, then explain."

**E2. Gao et al. (2023).** "PAL: Program-Aided Language Models." *ICML 2023*. [arXiv:2211.10435](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.10435)
Splits reasoning (LLM) from computation (a Python interpreter executes the LLM's code) — 72.0% on GSM8K vs. 56.9% for pure chain-of-thought, and a much larger gap on harder arithmetic (GSM-HARD). A template for offloading the *actual arithmetic* to a deterministic executor, exactly analogous to StudyHelp's sympy-backed verification layer.

**E3. Das, Banerjee, Aditya & Kulkarni (2024).** "MathSensei: A Tool-Augmented Large Language Model for Mathematical Reasoning." *NAACL 2024*. [arXiv:2402.17231](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.17231)
Combines an LLM with retrieval, Python/SymPy execution, and WolframAlpha. +13.5% over GPT-3.5-CoT on MATH. Notably, the Python module *underperforms specifically on elementary-level problems* — a useful, quantified caveat that even tool-augmented LLMs aren't perfectly reliable at exactly the arithmetic level a Class-5 tutor needs, reinforcing the case for a dedicated deterministic verifier rather than LLM-orchestrated tool-calling.

**E4. Davis & Aaronson (2023).** "Testing GPT-4 with Wolfram Alpha and Code Interpreter Plug-ins on Math and Science Problems." [arXiv:2308.05713](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.05713v3)
An important counterpoint: naively bolting a symbolic tool onto an LLM isn't sufficient, because the LLM can still mis-formulate the query sent to the tool ("interface failures"). Even GPT-4+WolframAlpha+CodeInterpreter still performs like "a middling undergraduate." Supports StudyHelp's specific design choice to keep verification *architecturally separate from and prior to* LLM control, rather than trusting the LLM to orchestrate its own tool calls.

**E5. Macina et al. (2025), MathTutorBench** — see C4. Cross-relevant here as empirical evidence that solving ability and teaching ability are distinct axes, supporting a modular (verify separately from explain) design over one monolithic LLM doing both.

**E6. Petukhova, Nguyen & Kochmar (2026)** — see C8. The clearest direct empirical validation available: giving an LLM tutor an *externally supplied* correctness signal and gold solution measurably improves remediation-dialogue quality over letting it self-judge correctness.

**E7. Liu et al. (2024), SocraticLM** — see C3. Useful here as a contrast case: a strong recent Socratic tutoring system with no described correctness-verification mechanism — the gap StudyHelp's architecture explicitly closes.

**E8. Hazra et al. (2026).** "SafeTutors: Benchmarking Pedagogical Safety in AI Tutoring Systems." [arXiv:2603.17373](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.17373v1)
Argues tutoring safety is categorically different from general LLM safety — the main risk is "quiet erosion of learning through answer over-disclosure," not toxicity. An 11-dimension/48-subrisk taxonomy and large benchmark (math, physics, chemistry) found every tested model exceeded 60% harm rate on multiple dimensions, and **harm rate surged from 17.7% to 77.8% across dialogue turns** — directly relevant given StudyHelp's multi-turn design. "Challenge" scores (pushing the student toward deeper reasoning rather than handing over the answer) were near zero across all tested models.

**E9. Park, Han, Yoo, Ahn, Yoon & Oh (2026).** "EduZone: A Framework for Evaluating LLM Safety for K-12 Students and Teachers." [arXiv:2608.02024](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.02024v1)
Education-specific red-teaming found dynamic multi-turn interactions are **1.8x more vulnerable** than single-turn, and generic jailbreak defenses transfer poorly to educational settings. Built around U.S. K-12 curriculum standards — a reminder that safety framing may need re-grounding for an NCERT/CBSE context specifically, not assumed to transfer.

**E10. Jiao, Afroogh, Chen, Murali, Atkinson & Dhurandhar (2025).** "LLMs and Childhood Safety: Identifying Risks and Proposing a Protection Framework for Safe Child-LLM Interaction." [arXiv:2502.11242](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.11242)
Systematic review (57 sources) of LLM risks specific to children. Two findings are directly load-bearing for StudyHelp: chatbot responses tend to reflect a 7th-grade reading level even when a 4th-grade (~Class 5) level is needed, and **children over-trust AI responses even when the AI is wrong**, due to anthropomorphization. This is strong support for two of StudyHelp's core design choices: never letting the LLM alone assert correctness to a child, and deliberately calibrating explanation language for a 10-year-old reading level rather than trusting default LLM register.

**E11 (flagged, not fully verified).** "MathEDU: Feedback Generation on Problem-Solving Processes for Mathematical Learning Support." [arXiv:2505.18056](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.18056). Confirmed to exist and be topically on-point (step-level, process-focused feedback generation) via two independent search passes, including a finding that fine-tuned models reach 95%+ on whole-solution correctness but only 24–41% exact-match on step-level error identification — but full methodological detail wasn't independently re-verified across all agents. Recommend a direct read before citing its specific numbers in a submitted paper.

## Synthesis: How this literature maps onto StudyHelp's design, and where the actual gap is

Pulling the five threads together, a consistent pattern shows up everywhere: the field is good at telling you *whether* a student is likely to get the next question right (knowledge tracing, threads A), reasonably good at *classifying* an error once a complete wrong answer or dialogue exists (thread B), rapidly improving at *generating* Socratic dialogue once an error is known (thread C), genuinely unsettled on *when* feedback timing helps most (thread D), and increasingly aware that trusting an LLM's own judgment of correctness — especially with children, especially over multiple dialogue turns — is risky (thread E). No paper found across all five threads combines all of the following in one system, evaluated with real primary-school-age children: (1) real-time detection at the exact step of an in-progress solution, not after a completed answer or dialogue turn, (2) deterministic/symbolic grounding of that detection rather than LLM self-judgment (which Srivatsa et al. 2025 and Jin et al. 2024 show LLMs are currently bad at), (3) diagnosis of the *specific* misconception rather than a bare right/wrong signal, (4) multi-turn Socratic guidance toward self-correction rather than answer-revealing, with explicit safeguards against the answer-leakage/over-disclosure failure mode SafeTutors documents getting worse over exactly this kind of extended dialogue, and (5) validation with actual Class-5/primary-age students rather than simulated students or older learners. The 2025 npj Science of Learning systematic review (A7) is the strongest single piece of evidence that this combination is genuinely underexplored, and Tutor CoPilot (C6) is the strongest evidence that the underlying hypothesis — earlier, better-targeted guidance improves real student outcomes — is at least directionally correct for a closely related population, even though it wasn't tested as a direct student-facing system. That combination is the specific, defensible gap StudyHelp's research contribution can claim.
